## From Recursive Self-Improvement to Recursive Co-Improvement      
_Questions are the clues to the unknown_      
   
Anushik Hovhannisyan      
Mar 18, 2026     
   
Our idea of progress is increasingly being tied to one loop: machine learning improving itself. That loop does not just promise better tooling, but also the gradual offloading of human cognition. Calls to slow down development are pushed aside by the claim that competitive pressure makes arriving late more dangerous than arriving first.     
     
The narrative is then treated as an inevitable path forward, even though it rests on the speculative assumption that the central loop must be machine self-improvement. But what if a better design is possible?     
     
_What if that loop is only part of a broader recursive process running between machine learning and human learning?_      
     
![Loop Visualizations](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/anushikhov/didactic-computing-machine/master/essays/img/1_Loop_Visualizations.jpg)      
   
When comparing artificial and human intelligence, the discussion often carries more certainty than our knowledge justifies. Considered separately, both reveal major unknowns and asymmetries that make the comparison premature. In machine learning, it remains unclear where current scaling trends lead. In human learning, the major unknown is the human brain, which is not yet fully understood.   
   
Human learning itself is also under-invested and still largely carried out through inherited systems. We speak as if machine self-improvement were the natural future of intelligence, even though we still lack a full understanding of the human mind and have barely begun to treat human learning as a process worth optimizing with comparable seriousness.   
   
Public discussion frequently settles on a superficial comparison between the estimated number of neurons in the human brain and the parameter counts of artificial neural networks. But that shortcut is much weaker than it sounds. Artificial neural networks are highly simplified mathematical abstractions inspired by biology; biological neurons remain far more complex computational objects than the units used in standard machine-learning models.    
   
Recent research still emphasizes that important neuronal features are missing from classical artificial neural networks, and that biological neurons remain much richer computational objects than the simplified units used in those models. Even at the level of basic mapping, our progress in neuroscience remains limited: the largest complete brain connectome produced so far is that of the adult female fruit fly, published in 2024, containing 139,255 neurons and 54.5 million synapses. That milestone is extraordinary, but a complete wiring diagram is still not the same thing as a full understanding of intelligence or learning. For that reason, using current progress in machine learning to draw conclusions about the limits or future of intelligence goes beyond what current evidence justifies. [1][2]      
   
Human learning is still largely carried out through inherited educational systems that were not originally designed to optimize learning itself. Their architecture remains dominated by rigid schedules, bureaucratic conditions, standardized pacing, and long interruptions that often ignore curiosity, readiness, or momentum.   
   
On top of this come the social and psychological obstacles that many learners know firsthand: bullying, discouragement, financial pressure, distraction, and the ordinary friction of institutions that sort and manage people more reliably than they help minds grow.   
   
Our mass learning systems are optimized for administration, standardization, and certification more than for actual human learning. What persists is not a serious large-scale design for human learning, but a structure that too often obstructs it. [3][4]   
   
There is already a large body of theory about human learning, but civilization does not seem to organize itself around optimizing it with anything like the same intensity, precision, or prestige as it does for machine learning.    
Both industry and academia work relentlessly to develop better architectures, training procedures, optimizers, and efficiency techniques for machine learning. The average human learner, by contrast, is still largely left to trial and error, private improvisation, and inherited institutional routines. There are volumes written on improving computing algorithms; the picture is not the same for human learning. Too often, success still depends on recitation, luck, and talent.   
   
For all the confidence surrounding progress in artificial intelligence, it remains possible that current machine learning trajectories will eventually slow, plateau, or encounter harder limits. If that happens, the long neglect of human learning will become even more exposed, because human intelligence will still need to carry judgment, redirection, and the next stage of progress. This is one reason why developing only one side of the loop is a fragile strategy.   
   
The most important contribution of machine learning may lie not in customized tutoring, simplified explanations, or, worse, the further neglect of learning through the outsourcing of thought, but in helping redesign human learning itself.   
   
Machine learning could help redesign human learning around understanding, pacing, retention, transfer, and fit. One possible contribution of ML to HL is a better science of distillation: identifying which representations, examples, sequences, and abstractions preserve depth while reducing confusion and wasted effort. That could mean new learning methods, better ways of structuring and categorizing the body of knowledge, and forms of compression that make deep understanding more reachable without flattening it. This bootstraps a process in which different kinds of intelligence improve themselves and each other.   
   
We have poured extraordinary effort into the recursive improvement of machine learning while leaving human learning inside inherited, badly optimized systems. But the future of intelligence does not have to be imagined as machine self-improvement alone. A broader loop is possible: one in which human learning and machine learning deepen both themselves and each other.
